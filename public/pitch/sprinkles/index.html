Section one

What we found.

The short version
  • 01Your website is Dawn, the free theme Shopify hands every new store, still on its first install. In your own words, not very sprinkle-y.
  • 02Text the link to anyone and the preview that arrives is black cursive on white. Not one color makes it.
  • 03Everyone who opens your homepage downloads 96 files that belong to the checkout, before anything is in the bag.
  • 04Your sugar page lists ten shades and sells none of them. Your workshops page has zero workshops. Your news page has zero news.
  • 05The site never says Marshall, Michigan. Not on any page.

First, what this is not. It is not about the desserts, the sugars, the tutorials or the person making them. The holographic finish is yours, six shops stock your sugar, and thousands of makers learned the method from you. The work is not the problem. What follows is about the website underneath it: how it is built, what it tells search engines, what it shows a stranger, and whose it is.

Eight measurements, taken from your live site on 3 September 2026. Every one came off sprinklessparklesbb.com itself and every one can be checked against it. We also ran the same accessibility audit we run on our own work against five of your pages, and the theme passes it with one exception, the pink announcement bar. That part is fine and this letter will not pretend otherwise.

Your website is the one Shopify gives away

The theme is Dawn, version 15.2.0, Shopify's free default, and your files live at /cdn/shop/t/1/: theme number one, the first ever installed on this store. Dawn is a fine starting point, which is exactly the trouble. It is the starting point for an enormous number of stores, and the whole point of your brand is that nothing else looks like it. The type is Shopify's default. The layout is Shopify's default. The colors on your site are the color of the page.

The link card is black and white

When somebody pastes your address into a text, Instagram or Facebook, the picture that shows up is the image your site names as its card. Yours is the logo file: black script on a white field, and it drops the BB. Nothing holographic, no sugar, no color. For a business whose entire pitch is how it looks, the first thing anyone sees of it is a signature on a blank page. The tag also points at it over plain http:// on a site that is otherwise https.

Your homepage downloads the checkout

Open the front page and, in the background, the browser fetches 96 files that belong to the payment page: 74 scripts and 22 stylesheets from Shopify's checkout, for every visitor, before anyone has put anything in the bag. The page itself carries 50 script tags and 29 stylesheets. None of that is yours to switch off. It is what the platform does on every page, every time, regardless of who is typing.

The same ten-shade block is pasted a second time onto the Holographic Cake Info page, underneath the Frost Form instructions, where it has nothing to do with the cake.

Nowhere on the site is Marshall

We read fifteen pages. Not one of them says Marshall, or Michigan, or where the sugar ships from. The business record your store gives search engines has three facts in it, name, logo and web address, and four empty strings where links should be. The contact page is a form with no email address and no phone number on it; the email appears only in the FAQ and the refund policy. A person who wants to book you for a class, or a shop that wants to stock you, has to dig.

Things a person should not have to remember

The bar across the top of every page says Holographic Dessert Sheets Now Available! The sheets were added to your store on 22 March 2025, by your own product record, so they have been newly available for eighteen months. The cake pop tutorial, listed in February 2025, still wears a New Product! badge, names its seller as MY STORE, and tells buyers Shipping calculated at checkout for a file that downloads. Its browser title reads Holographic-Cake-Pop-Tutorial, hyphens included. The homepage title is just your name, and the Products, Contact and News pages have no description for Google at all. None of this is neglect. On a rented store every one of those is a box a person has to remember to go and untick.

Your desserts are the most photographed thing in this corner of the internet. Your website is the least.

Everything above is a symptom. The real finding is the gap. Open your Instagram and it is oil-slick cake pops, rainbow sugar and a finish people travel to learn. Open your website and it is a default theme, a grey hero and a form. The quality on the screen has never matched the quality on the stick. A brand this visual deserves a website built to its own standard, and closing that gap is the entire reason this letter exists.

Why did the gap happen? Not neglect of the craft, plainly. A rented store that is awkward to make your own, so it stayed the way it came out of the box.

Section two

The part that matters most.

What you have now

Your store runs on a Shopify subscription. The theme is theirs, the checkout is theirs, the hosting is theirs, and Powered by Shopify sits in your footer. The day you stop paying, the store closes. What you can take with you is spreadsheets: your products, your customers and your orders as files. Not a website that can be switched back on somewhere else.

None of that is improper. It is how renting works. It is worth knowing which parts apply to you.

What we would do instead

You own it. All of it.

When the build fee is paid, the site is yours in writing: design, source code, content, accounts. Your domain stays in your name. Month to month after launch, thirty days' notice, no penalty. If you ever leave, you take everything with you, working.

And it looks like the work

The first concept we showed you came back too dark, and you were right. What is live now is lighter, simpler and multicolored: confetti cake and sprinkles, sparkle as you scroll, your own photographs doing the talking. A site that could only be yours, which is the one thing a theme can never be.

Three questions worth asking any platform

1. If we stop paying you, what do we keep? 2. Can our store look like nothing else on your platform? 3. Who do we call when something needs changing? Our answers are: everything, yes, and Kevin. Ask Shopify the same three.

Built to a standard, and here is how we know

None of this is a mockup. It is a working site, and we measured it rather than describing it.

  • It carries a third of the code. 150 KB of JavaScript against 453 KB on your current homepage, measured on the same deliberately slow phone profile, because that is the phone at the craft fair. Less code is less for a weak signal to drag down.
  • It works for people who do not see well. Every size and color on it was measured against the accessibility standard at four screen widths, rather than eyeballed.
  • It works with the modern parts switched off. Turn off JavaScript and the shop still lists everything. Nobody gets a blank page.
  • Nothing rented on top. No theme license, no app subscriptions, no per-feature upsell. The price at the end of this letter is the price.

Two things in the demo are stand-ins and say so. The sugar prices and the shipping rate are placeholders until you tell us the real ones, and the tutorial files are not attached yet, so a test order says "emailed to you shortly" instead of pretending.

Section four

The part your website is missing.

You make the sugar. Six other shops sell it. Your own site does not.

Ten blends, a photograph of each, and not one of them can be bought where they were made.

What we did about it. Every shade has its own page on the demo, with a jar, a price and a bag, and it ships from Michigan. If your agreements with the six shops keep direct sales off the table, the shelf comes out and the page still looks like the product. If they do not, every jar sold from your own page is a sale nobody else takes a share of, and the customer's name and email are yours.

The same goes for the workshops. Dates on your own site, a list that hears about them first, and a button for anyone who wants to host one. Today a person who wants to book you has to find an email address in the refund policy.

And the list itself. A signup on the homepage, and every address it collects belongs to you, exportable any time. On a rented store the customer list is one of the things that travels badly.
